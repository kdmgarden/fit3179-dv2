FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 · Diabetes in Australia

1.35 million Australians live with diabetes.
Who are they, where do they live, and is it getting more common?

Diabetes means the body struggles to control the sugar in the blood. Most people have type 2, which is linked to age, weight and family history. This page walks through five questions using the latest national health surveys.

  • 1 in 19Australians (5.3%)

  • Nearly 9 in 10have type 2

1

Is diabetes getting more common?

Yes. The share of people reporting diabetes rose from 3.3% in 2001 to 5.3% in 2022. The number of people more than doubled.

Part of that rise is simply an older population, because diabetes is more common with age. The age-adjusted rate removes that effect. It asks what the rate would be if Australia always had the same mix of ages.

That rate still climbed, from 3.4% to 4.5%. But it has not risen since 2014–15. Almost half of the rise in the raw rate comes from Australia getting older.

A second source agrees. The AIHW reports that people registered with the National Diabetes Services Scheme grew from about 460,000 in 2000 to 1.3 million in 2021. It also reports that new diagnoses have fallen since peaking in 2008.

2

Where in Australia is it most common?

South Australia has the highest raw rate at 7.2%. The Australian Capital Territory has the lowest at 3.4%.

Hover over a state for details. Use the switch on the map to compare the raw rate with the age-adjusted rate.

2b

Why do some states change when we adjust for age?

South Australia and Tasmania have older populations, which pushes their raw rates up. The tile map shows by how much.

The Northern Territory is the opposite. Its population is younger, so its rate rises once age is adjusted for.

3

Who gets diabetes: age, sex and type

Under 35, diabetes is rare. After 55 it becomes common, and nearly 1 in 5 people aged 75 and over has it.

From age 35, men are ahead of women in every age group. For ages 65–74 the rates are 17.1% for men and 10.5% for women.

The climb is almost all type 2. Type 1 usually starts in childhood or young adulthood. It stays below 2% at every age.

4

Does where you live and your circumstances matter?

They do. Adults in the most disadvantaged areas are 3.5 times as likely to have diabetes as adults in the least disadvantaged areas (10.3% against 2.9%).

Outside the big cities the rate is higher too. This is mostly because of men.

4b

Which groups stand out from the average?

Each dot is a group. The grey tick is the average for everyone being compared: 5.3% for all ages, or 6.5% for people aged 15 and over in the work and study groups. Red means higher than average, and blue means lower.

Read this chart with care. Many groups are partly a stand-in for age. People who live alone or are outside the workforce include many retirees. People who arrived in the last 10 years tend to be younger.

5

What else goes with diabetes?

Diabetes rarely travels alone. Among people with diabetes, 41% also have arthritis, 35% a mental health condition and 24% heart, stroke or vascular disease. Nearly half (47%) have three or more of the ten long-term conditions the survey asked about, counting diabetes itself.

Body measurements show a steep pattern. 3.8% of adults of healthy weight have diabetes. This rises to 18.8% for the most severe obesity category. These are links, not proof of cause, and they mostly describe type 2.

6

So what does it all add up to?

Three things to take away from the surveys.

Older, not just more

The raw rate rose from 3.3% to 5.3%. Take out ageing and the rise is smaller (3.4% to 4.5%). It has been flat since 2014–15.

Age, sex and place matter

Rates climb steeply after 55. Men lead women from 35. Adults in the most disadvantaged areas are 3.5 times as likely to have diabetes as those in the least.

Read state gaps with care

Only South Australia and the ACT are clearly different from the national rate. Other gaps come from age mix or are too small for a survey to be sure.

About the data

Sources

  • Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS), National Health Survey 2022, data tables 1–5 and 16. Self-reported, plus some measurements.
  • Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), Diabetes: Australian facts (page updated September 2026), using the ABS National Health Measures Survey 2022–24. Based on blood tests.
  • State boundaries: tonywr71/GeoJson-Data (simplified).

Words used

  • Raw rate: the share of all people in a group who have diabetes.
  • Age-adjusted rate: the raw rate recalculated as if every group had the same mix of ages, so groups can be compared fairly.
  • Margin of error: the range the true figure probably sits within, because a survey asks only some people.

Limits

  • The ABS 2022 figures include type 1, type 2 and type not known, and people who said they once had diabetes.
  • The two surveys use different methods, so their numbers are shown side by side but not mixed in one chart.
  • The ABS adds small random changes to tables to protect privacy.
